This project was the final project for my computer vision class (EECE 5639). I had become interested in diffusion models, and what drew me to [PLONK](https://nicolas-dufour.github.io/plonk) from Dufour et al. is that it reframes image geolocalization as a diffusion process on the surface of the Earth rather than as classification or single-point regression. That formulation lets the model return a _distribution_ of plausible locations for an image — which matches how a person actually solves these problems (an image of a soccer field could be any soccer field on Earth) and is genuinely useful for downstream applications like verifying media provenance, where a probabilistic region is more honest than a brittle single guess. The goal of the project was to identify and test concrete improvements to that architecture.

I built on the original PLONK implementation, kept the diffusion backbone fixed, and trained on the OSV-5M dataset. The model uses Riemannian Flow Matching on a spherical manifold, which extends standard flow matching to operate on arbitrary manifolds — a natural fit here, since geolocalization is fundamentally a 2D problem on the surface of Earth rather than a 3D one in its volume. With the diffusion architecture held constant, the experimental variables were the image encoder (the conditioning signal $c$) and how multiple images were fused into that conditioning. All training and evaluation ran on a single RTX 4090.

I organized the experiments around two questions:

**1. Does the choice of image encoder matter, and does it have to be a geographically pretrained one?** PLONK's default is StreetCLIP, which is explicitly pretrained for geographic context. I compared it against DINOv2 (a self-supervised spatial-reconstruction encoder) and MobileCLIP2-S4 (a heavily compressed CLIP variant designed for on-device use). StreetCLIP achieved the best mean Haversine distance at 1,076 km, closely matching the original paper's 1,069 km baseline. DINOv2 came in at 1,330 km — worse at small radii but only modestly behind at larger ones, consistent with the idea that DINOv2 captures spatial structure but lacks the symbolic/textual grounding (street signs, scripts) that CLIP-aligned encoders pick up. The more interesting result was MobileCLIP2-S4: at 1,597 km mean distance and a 768-dim embedding (vs. 1024 for the other two), it landed within a few percent of DINOv2 across most accuracy bands and within 0.1% on city-level accuracy, despite being a fraction of the size.

**2. Does fusing multiple proximal images improve predictions over a single anchor image?** I built a proximity-grouping pipeline that projects every dataset coordinate onto a 3D unit sphere, builds a KD-tree over the points, and queries it at a chord-length threshold corresponding to a 100 m surface radius — yielding, for each image, the set of other images captured nearby. During training, an anchor image is augmented with a randomly sampled subset of its neighbors and their precomputed embeddings are fused into the conditioning vector. I tested three fusion strategies: averaging the embeddings, an attention-based combination (single-head transformer with the anchor as query and the neighbors as keys/values), and randomly selecting one embedding per diffusion step. Across both averaging and attention, more neighbors _worsened_ the mean distance error and accuracy, but _improved_ coverage and precision/recall against the ground-truth manifold. In other words, fusion spread predictions over a wider, more representative geographic region rather than tightening them around the true point — fine-grained details were getting averaged away, and a few far-flung guesses inflated the mean distance even when most predictions were reasonable. A likely cause is dataset structure: only 0.28% of training images have 5+ neighbors, and a lot of OSV-5M comes from dashcams pointed in the same direction, so proximal images often add little new information.

A few things stood out beyond the headline numbers. The OSV-5M dataset is heavily Western-biased — the USA and Europe together account for roughly 60% of the training data — so the model is unlikely to generalize well to underrepresented regions. Validation loss continued to drop after training loss had plateaued around 850k steps, which I attribute to the learning-rate scheduler reaching its low point. And the attention-based fusion didn't actually outperform plain averaging; it appeared to suffer from the same data scarcity, since the model rarely saw enough neighbor-rich samples during training to learn a useful weighting.

If I were to continue this work, the priorities would be: fine-tuning the vision encoders during diffusion training rather than relying on frozen pretrained weights (especially for DINOv2, which might close the geographic gap if it could learn geolocalization-specific features), and moving to a substantially larger and denser dataset like YFCC100M so the multi-image fusion experiments aren't bottlenecked by the sparsity of proximity clusters in OSV-5M.
